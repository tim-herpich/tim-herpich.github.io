Beyond Beta: Hunting for the Perfect Portfolio

1 Jan 2024 3 min read Tim Herpich

Six Decades of Capital Market Research

The question of what constitutes an optimal investment portfolio has been at the heart of capital market research for decades. Traditionally, the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), developed by William Sharpe and based on Harry Markowitz’s Modern Portfolio Theory, posits that the market portfolio—a fully diversified basket of all investable assets—represents the most efficient investment. According to CAPM, expected returns depend solely on systematic market risk (beta), meaning individual investors cannot achieve excess returns through stock selection or market timing. However, empirical studies have repeatedly challenged this assumption, leading to the emergence of factor investing.

Factor investing builds upon CAPM by identifying additional risk premia beyond market beta. The pioneering work of Eugene Fama and Kenneth French introduced the three-factor model, which incorporates not just market risk but also size (small-cap stocks outperforming large caps) and value (stocks with high book-to-market ratios generating excess returns). Later, Mark Carhart extended this to a four-factor model by including momentum—the tendency of stocks that have performed well in the past to continue outperforming. Further refinements introduced the quality factor (Robert Novy-Marx), which rewards companies with strong profitability metrics, and even broader multi-factor models aiming to capture persistent return drivers.

The Problem

While factor investing has historically been supported by empirical data, recent years have seen a significant underperformance of key factor portfolios—particularly value stocks—compared to simple market-cap-weighted index funds. This has led to renewed debates about the long-term persistence of factor premia and whether these excess returns were merely historical artifacts rather than robust market inefficiencies. The so-called post-publication effect suggests that once a factor is widely recognized and exploited, its return premium may disappear due to arbitrage and increased investor participation.

Analysis and Conclusions

In light of these debates, the question arises: should a retail investor still follow the market-neutral approach of CAPM or can factor-based strategies indeed offer superior risk-adjusted returns? The central conclusion of the paper is that, while the empirical evidence does not conclusively rule out the continued existence of certain factor premia, their practical exploitability for retail investors is severely constrained. Transaction costs, turnover, tax inefficiencies, model instability, and regulatory frictions significantly erode the theoretical excess returns suggested by academic models. In particular, once factors are widely disseminated and incorporated into investable products, their performance tends to weaken, consistent with the post-publication effect observed in the literature. As a result, the paper argues that a broadly diversified, market-cap-weighted buy-and-hold portfolio—designed to be cost-efficient, tax-efficient, and robust across market regimes—remains the most reliable and economically sound investment strategy for retail investors.
